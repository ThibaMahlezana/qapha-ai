How Qapha works

What Qapha is for, how it is built, what we tested and what we found, including what did not work.

The ultimate goal

Qapha aims to become a general adaptive reasoning system for physical environments: a system that can watch an unfamiliar scene, work out how things in it behave, predict what will happen, say how certain it is, notice when it is wrong, and improve. In the long run it should also answer "what if?" questions and decide for itself which observation or experiment would teach it most.

That matters wherever a machine must act before things happen: robot arms, drones, warehouses, farms, factories.

How the experiments lead there

A general reasoner cannot be built in one step, so we climb a ladder of capabilities. Each rung is a capability the final system needs, tested with an experiment that can fail. The bouncing ball is the test bench for the lower rungs.

See. Find and follow an object in real video (SAM 3.1).
Predict honestly. Seal every guess before the answer is revealed, so results cannot be faked.
Test fairly. Pre-register experiments; judge only on data nobody has analysed.
Show the reasoning. A lab where every guess, hit and miss is visible.
Know how unsure it is. Predict zones, not just points, and check they are truthful.
Learn about the scene. Infer a hidden fact (where the floor is) from what it saw, and use it to anticipate events.
Answer "what if?" Predict a drop from 150 cm after watching 50 cm and 100 cm, then compare with the real clip.
Choose what to observe. Spend expensive perception only where it reduces uncertainty most.
Generalise. Other objects, other scenes, live cameras, robots.

The rungs are general even though the test bench is a ball. "Learn that a floor exists and expect a bounce" is the same skill as "learn that a table edge exists and expect a cup to fall". The experiments are built to transfer.

Why a bouncing ball

Smooth is easy, sudden is hard

A falling ball is predictable. At the floor everything reverses in one frame. Every model we tested failed hardest at that moment.

The robot problem in miniature

Grasping, collisions, slipping and stopping are all sudden changes. If a system cannot anticipate a bounce, it cannot anticipate contact.

The environment matters

A bounce happens because of the floor. To anticipate it, Qapha must learn something about the scene, not just the ball.

Cheap and checkable

Anyone can film it with a phone, repeat it, and see whether a prediction was right.

The predict-and-check loop

ObserveSAM finds the ball
Predictevery model guesses the next frame
Sealthe guess is fingerprinted
Revealthe real frame arrives
Scoreerror and surprise measured
Learnmodels and floor memory update

The rule that makes this trustworthy: a prediction for a frame may only use earlier frames. Each guess is sealed with a SHA-256 fingerprint first, and automated tests check that changing a future frame never changes a past guess.

How Meta AI is used

Meta modelRole in QaphaWhy there
SAM 3.1
via the Meta Model API		 Given a phrase such as "blue ball", it finds and tracks the object through every frame, returning a box and mask per frame. Perception is the hardest part to build by hand; SAM works on any describable object without training on our footage.
Muse Spark
reasoning model		 Reasons over structured evidence, for example whether a reversal near the floor might be a collision, and proposes what to observe next. It may only cite evidence we measured, and may not claim verified contact. Weighing competing explanations is where a reasoning model helps; a validator keeps it honest.

Design choice: predictions, uncertainty and scores are transparent mathematics, not language-model output, so every number is reproducible and testable. Meta's models do what they are best at: seeing and reasoning about evidence.

Knowing how unsure it is

  • Coverage: a 90% zone should contain the ball about 90% of the time.
  • Surprise score (NLL): heavily punishes confident misses and rewards an honest "I'm not sure". Lower is better.

Finding: a standard Kalman filter's zone settles to about 15 px and never changes. At bounces it misses by around 100 px while still claiming 15. In the lab, the dashed zone-size line stays flat while the error spikes.

Learning the floor

The bounce-aware model watches the ball's bottom edge. When it goes down then up, the model records "there is a floor here". Near that height it keeps two competing hypotheses, "keeps falling" and "bounces", weighted by the chance of contact. The lab shows both as coloured zones with percentages, and the learned floor as a dashed line.

  • Frames miss the moment of contact, so the lowest point seen is above the true floor; the model keeps the deepest bounce seen.
  • Bounciness is assumed (0.6); estimating it from the two frames around contact gave nonsense.

Keeping the science honest

Development vs test

Methods are built on videos we have studied, and judged only on clips nobody had analysed.

Pre-registration

Code fingerprints, settings, hypotheses and pass/fail rules are sealed before a test. The evaluation refuses to run if locked code changes.

Nothing dropped silently

Untrackable clips are reported as ineligible, with the reason.

Failures are kept

Every failed hypothesis stays on the record, including our own protocol mistakes.

What failed and why

  • The original adaptive learner lost to a one-line baseline: choosing the right model mattered more than switching between models.
  • The bounce detector is too jumpy. Any 1-pixel down-then-up counts, so wobble can create a false floor that misleads later predictions. Seen on two independent clips; it is the clearest next fix.
  • Footage quality. Clips were compressed by WhatsApp; in one far shot the ball was too small for SAM to find.
  • Our own protocol. Test v1 analysed the wrong part of each clip. It is recorded, and v2 changed only the protocol, never the method.

Limitations

  • Positions are in image pixels, not metres; scale differs between clips.
  • Predictions are one frame ahead (1/25 or 1/30 s).
  • One tracked object, recorded video (not live), and a small number of clips.
  • Contact is inferred from motion, never physically verified.
  • Fingerprints show a file was not changed; they do not prove when it was created.

What comes next

  1. A sturdier bounce detector: require a real fall before believing in a floor.
  2. Gravity-aware motion, so the model knows when the ball reaches the floor.
  3. "What if?": use the 50 and 100 cm drops to predict the 150 cm drop (the ruler converts pixels to centimetres).
  4. Choosing what to observe: call SAM only when uncertainty is high, since perception costs money.
  5. Live camera input and more than one object.

How to read the lab

  • White box and dot: where SAM saw the ball.
  • Coloured rings: each model's guess, made before the frame was shown; dashed lines show the miss.
  • Amber ellipse: the Kalman 90% zone; red when the ball lands outside it.
  • Teal and pink ellipses: the bounce-aware model's two hypotheses, with probabilities.
  • Dashed teal line: the floor learned from an earlier bounce.
  • Diamond at the edge: a guess that fell outside the picture.
  • Chart: error per frame. Click to jump; arrow keys step; space plays.
